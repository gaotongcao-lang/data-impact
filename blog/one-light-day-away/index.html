One Light-Day Away

What Is the Point of Research?

Delphine PA Dang

A few days ago I submitted my first manuscript. I expected relief. Instead a question arrived and stayed: what is the point of research, of studying, of thinking, of writing?

Every year, so many honours and PhD students graduate. Why did they begin? Is the journey only the first class, the paper, the acceptance? I am probably too young to answer. But I know what a first class is. It is a verdict on the work. It measures our capability to perform a task; it does not see us as human beings who think for ourselves.

Hannah Arendt draws this line sharply. There is what a person is: their “qualities, gifts, talents, and shortcomings.”1 And there is who a person is, which appears only when they speak and act for themselves. A grade can measure the what. The who escapes every rubric. It shows in the question we choose to pursue, and in the position we defend when no one asked us to.

We will all die one day. A life spent proving what we are seems a strange use of something that ends. And yet we want to be remembered. Perhaps that is why we keep doing more. Perhaps it is why, in 1977, we fastened a golden record to Voyager and sent it beyond the solar system.2

The sky has answered with silence. The astrophysicist Michael Garrett offers one explanation: perhaps civilisations build artificial intelligence and fail to govern it in time, so none lasts much.3 My own paper studies how we measure AI, so the thought stays with me. If no one lasts, who is left to remember?

Then the record was never really for remembering. Its makers knew it would almost certainly never be played.4 Its worth lay in the asking: what is worth saying about us? It was not a test to pass. It was a disclosure of who we are, sent with no examiner waiting.

Arendt meets the fear of death in the same spirit. We must die, she writes, yet we “are not born in order to die but in order to begin.”5 Each of us arrives as someone new, able to start what has never been started. Research, at its best, is one of those beginnings.

On 18 November, Voyager 1 will be one light-day from Earth, forty-nine years after leaving.6 A signal from home will take a whole day to reach it. Its instruments are falling silent.7 It carries its record on regardless, toward no listener.

Maybe that is enough. Not to be graded, not even to be remembered, but to begin, and in beginning, to show who we are.
